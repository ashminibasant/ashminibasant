# Marketing & Consumer Trust

A practical review framework for helping marketing, product, growth, legal, and compliance teams move faster without creating avoidable consumer risk.

This portfolio section is based on generalized professional experience with regulated marketing, customer communications, digital channels, partner practices, product launches, and consumer-protection requirements. It does not disclose confidential employer or client information and is not legal advice.

## The Core Question

The most useful review is not simply, “Is this compliant?”

It is:

> What is the business trying to say or achieve, what could a reasonable consumer understand from it, what evidence supports the claim, and what has to change before launch?

That framing keeps compliance from becoming a late-stage blocker and turns it into a launch discipline.

## Campaign Review Flow

1. **Business objective** — What action, belief, or behavior is the campaign trying to drive?
2. **Audience** — Who will see it, and are there vulnerable, regulated, or differently situated consumer groups?
3. **Channel** — Website, email, social, paid media, SEO, partner content, landing page, offer, in-product messaging, or sales collateral.
4. **Primary claim** — What is the main promise or impression?
5. **Evidence** — What facts, product behavior, terms, data, or substantiation support the claim?
6. **Disclosure analysis** — What must be clear, prominent, timely, and understandable?
7. **Customer journey** — Does the experience after the ad or claim match what the consumer was led to expect?
8. **Partner risk** — Is a third party, creator, affiliate, reseller, or co-marketing partner changing the message or experience?
9. **Approval and evidence** — Who approved the final version and what was retained?
10. **Post-launch monitoring** — What complaints, conversion anomalies, or customer-friction signals should trigger review?

## Claims & Disclosures Review Matrix

| Review Area | Questions |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Is the statement literally true? |
| Net Impression | Could the overall message still mislead even if individual words are technically accurate? |
| Substantiation | What evidence supports the claim? |
| Material Terms | What conditions would matter to a consumer's decision? |
| Prominence | Is the disclosure noticeable and understandable in the actual channel? |
| Timing | Does the consumer see the disclosure before making the relevant decision? |
| Consistency | Do landing pages, sales scripts, product screens, and partner materials tell the same story? |
| Audience | Would a reasonable member of the target audience interpret the claim differently? |
| Product Reality | Does the product or service perform as advertised? |
| Evidence Retention | Can the organization later show what was reviewed and approved? |

## Digital Channel Risk Review

### Website & Landing Pages

Review headline claims, pricing/offer language, qualification criteria, calls to action, testimonials, disclosures, comparison claims, accessibility, and whether the next step matches the promise.

### Email & Lifecycle Marketing

Review subject lines, sender identity, offer terms, urgency language, consent/opt-out handling, triggered messaging, and whether segmentation creates unintended customer-treatment issues.

### Social & Paid Media

Review abbreviated claims, visual impressions, creator/partner content, disclosure placement, dynamic variants, and whether context changes the meaning of approved copy.

### SEO & Search Content

Review whether titles, snippets, FAQs, comparison pages, and educational content create claims or expectations that differ from the underlying product or service.

### Partner & Co-Marketing

Review who controls the message, who approves changes, disclosure ownership, brand use, data-sharing implications, handoff experience, complaint ownership, and escalation rules.

## Launch Readiness Checklist

- [ ] Primary claim is supported
- [ ] Material limitations are identified
- [ ] Disclosures are understandable in the actual channel
- [ ] Customer journey matches the marketing promise
- [ ] Pricing, eligibility, timing, and conditions are consistent across assets
- [ ] Partner/affiliate materials have approval rules
- [ ] Final versions are retained
- [ ] Named approver is documented
- [ ] Complaint/monitoring signals are defined
- [ ] High-risk ambiguity is resolved before launch

## Risk-Based Review

Not every asset needs the same level of scrutiny. I would increase review depth when a campaign includes:

* financial or health consequences
* individualized or eligibility-related claims
* comparative or superiority claims
* scarcity or urgency
* partner/affiliate distribution
* automated or AI-generated personalization
* vulnerable audiences
* new product functionality
* pricing or material contractual terms
* a history of consumer complaints or confusing journeys

## Example: Fictional Launch Review

**Scenario:** A consumer platform wants to advertise, “Get approved in minutes.”

**Questions I would ask:**

* Does “approved” mean final approval, prequalification, eligibility screening, or something else?
* What percentage of users actually receive a decision within minutes?
* Are additional verification steps common?
* Do partner channels use the same meaning?
* Does the landing page immediately clarify any conditions?
* Could the claim create an expectation that conflicts with the real customer journey?

**Possible outcome:** Keep the speed advantage but narrow the claim to what the evidence actually proves, align landing-page language, add a clear condition where needed, and monitor abandonment/complaint data after launch.

## What Good Looks Like

A strong marketing-compliance function should help teams launch faster because the rules of the road are clear before creative is finished. The goal is not to make marketing timid. It is to make the strongest possible claim that the organization can support, deliver, and defend.
